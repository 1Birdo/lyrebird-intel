1Checking an address?

If an abuse report linked you here, the fastest answer is the indicator feed. Every address in the current window is in it, and the table in section 9 lists the most active.

recompute this
curl -s https://raw.githubusercontent.com/1Birdo/lyrebird-intel/main/iocs/source-ips.csv | grep '^203.0.113.9,'

Full feed: iocs/source-ips.csv · per-address write-ups: incidents/

What an entry here does and does not mean. It means this address opened a connection to a decoy and did the things recorded against it, at the times recorded. It does not mean the owner did it, that the address is still hostile, or that it should be blocked without your own corroboration — addresses are frequently reassigned and frequently behind NAT.

Absence means very little either. This is one sensor. Not finding an address here says only that this decoy did not see it.

Lyrebird publishes no blocklist, blocks nobody, and sells nothing. If you believe a record is wrong — misattribution, timestamps that cannot correspond to traffic your host originated, or a large-scale NAT egress where the behaviour has stopped — open an issue on the repository with the address and we will correct or annotate it. A compromised device on your own network is not grounds for withdrawal, but the incident page will carry a dated remediation note on request.

3There is no night

First sighting of each address, by hour of day (UTC). The quietest hour of the day still brought 314 first-time addresses; the busiest brought 474. Hour of day says something about the botnets' schedulers and nothing about where anyone is.

4What they think this is

None of these attackers know what this machine is. They are guessing — and they mostly guess a fibre modem or a decade-old DVR, because the credentials and paths they choose only make sense against those devices.

10Method

Every figure above is computed from the published CSVs, and every table carries the command that reproduces it. In order:

  • Events — the per-day event counts for addresses seen in the window. One attacker action can emit several events, so this is never labelled 'attacks'.
  • Source addresses — distinct public addresses. Private, loopback, reserved, multicast and link-local sources are excluded before anything is recorded.
  • The funnel — each bar counts addresses meeting a strictly stronger condition than the one above it.
  • Credential attempts and distinct pairs are reported separately and never merged; conflating them once overstated this corpus by a wide margin.
  • Payload hosts exclude addresses that are not routable from the internet. Mirai templates the victim's own LAN gateway into its fetch, so counting those would put an unreachable address at the top.
  • Countries come from an offline table and are counted per address.

Some figures are lifetime totals for addresses active in this window rather than totals for the window itself — a per-address rollup covers the whole retention period. Credential attempts, sessions and payload fetches are of that kind, and no trend is ever computed by subtracting two of them.

What is deliberately not published

Anything the sensor said back. No banners, no fake command output, no filesystem contents, no prompts. A transcript of the emulation's replies is a fingerprinting cookbook — publish it and anyone can build a reliable detector for this honeypot, after which it stops seeing anything worth publishing. The guarantee is structural: the generator only ever reads attacker-supplied fields.

Also absent: anything describing the deployment — its address, host, provider, software versions, listening-port inventory or uptime. That describes the sensor, not the attacker.

An admission against interest: aggregate statistics are themselves a weak fingerprint of this sensor. That is the price of publishing anything at all. It is far weaker than publishing responses would be, and it is a reason never to add response-side metrics later on the grounds that "we already publish statistics".
